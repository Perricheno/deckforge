Sources of Competitive Advantage in the Age of AI: Technology or Complementary Capabilities?

Is competitive advantage produced by AI technology itself, or by complementary capabilities such as leadership, workflows, talent, culture and governance?

Presented byAmangeldy Shyngyskhan
 Zhanerke Oryngazina
Abstract

Generative AI is available to almost every firm through the same cloud providers, at falling prices. If access is universal, it cannot by itself explain why some organizations earn superior returns from AI. Drawing on the resource-based view, dynamic capabilities and the economics of general-purpose technologies, we argue that AI is a necessary but not sufficient condition: durable advantage arises from the firm-specific complements that determine how AI is used, scaled and governed.

Background and problem02 / 06

1AI access is becoming a commodity

A company gets a lasting advantage from a resource only if it is valuable, rare, hard to copy and well organized inside the company. Three trends weaken the first three conditions for AI technology itself:

  • Convergence. Leading model providers trade places at the frontier and quality gaps keep narrowing.
  • Falling cost. The price of a given capability declines for every buyer, lowering barriers to entry.
  • Open access. The same APIs, cloud platforms and open models are available to competitors within weeks.

The same happened with IT before: when everyone has the same technology, it becomes basic infrastructure and stops making one company different from another.

Theory and historical evidence03 / 06

2New technologies pay off through complements

History of big technologies shows the same pattern: the technology arrives first, the gains arrive later. Value appears only after companies invest in organization, processes and skills around it.

  • Complementarity. Things that are weak alone become strong together, so copying one element rarely copies the result.
  • Intangibles. Unmeasured investment in process and skills depresses measured productivity at first, then lifts it. This is called the productivity J-curve.
  • Dynamic capabilities. Advantage comes from the ability to combine and rebuild resources as technology changes.

Framework and propositions04 / 06

3AI is the core; complements make it valuable

P1

Access to AI alone does not yield sustained advantage.

Because the technology is widely available and converging, it is neither rare nor hard to copy.

P2

The value of AI increases with the strength of complements.

Returns are multiplicative rather than additive: with no complements the return approaches zero, with strong ones it multiplies.

P3

Complements are firm-specific and accumulate slowly.

They are embedded in routines, relationships and history, so imitation is slow and costly. This is what makes the advantage durable.

Conclusion

AI is a necessary but not sufficient condition. Durable advantage is produced mainly by the complementary capabilities that make AI usable, scalable and trusted.

Implication 1

Budget for process redesign, training and governance alongside licences.

Implication 2

Make leaders accountable for adoption outcomes, not the number of pilots.

Implication 3

Build learning loops so every deployment adds knowledge rivals cannot copy.
