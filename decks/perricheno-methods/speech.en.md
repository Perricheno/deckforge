# Speech: Perricheno methods (about 3.5 minutes)

## Slide 1
Perricheno is a Telegram service that turns a source document into a compiled LaTeX document through an agent pipeline, but there is still no evidence about its results or about how students actually see it. Our aim is to measure those results and to explain why students keep using the service, so our research question is whether an agent-based document generator improves the efficiency, quality and usefulness of academic writing, and what explains continued use.

## Slide 2
In our framework, the user context influences how the service is used, use leads to outcomes such as efficiency, quality and perception, and these outcomes decide whether students continue to use it. To study this, we chose applied research with a quantitative survey over three months, combined with usage logs, because short closed questions in the bot reach many students quickly, standard scales give comparable numbers, and the logs let us check what students say against what they actually do. We did not choose interviews or mixed methods because of limited time. With this approach, we expect to learn how much time the service saves per document, which perceptions drive repeat use, how different groups compare, and which low-rated items should be fixed first, while accepting that a non-random sample and self-reported answers mean we can report associations but not causes.

## Slide 3
We formulated four hypotheses, and each one is tied to a specific test. First, documents are produced faster than in the students' usual workflow, which we test with the Wilcoxon test. Second, higher usefulness and ease of use go with repeated use, which we test with logistic regression. Third, fewer compile errors go with higher satisfaction, which we test with Spearman correlation. Fourth, satisfaction differs by prior experience and plan tier, which we test with the Kruskal-Wallis test. To test them, we collect three kinds of data: usage logs and Google Analytics, which show what students do, survey answers about ratings, time saved, usability, trust and intention to continue, which show what students say, and response-quality data such as completion time, which helps us remove careless answers. All of these records are joined by a hashed ID, so we can compare what students say with what they do without storing personal data.

## Slide 4
Our sample is about one hundred students over about three months, and they receive free access to a paid plan as thanks for taking part. Because our goal is to collect as many responses as possible, a thirty-second micro-survey appears inside the bot after each document, with one-tap questions about usefulness, time saved and whether the document compiled on the first try. At the end, a final survey of about three minutes uses the System Usability Scale together with short scales for usefulness, ease of use, trust and intention to continue, and we keep the response rate high by staying inside the chat, keeping the survey under three minutes and sending two reminders.

## Slide 5
We use AI agents not only to confirm our hypotheses but also to find hidden problems in the responses. A cleaning agent first flags speeders, duplicates and missing data, then code computes every score and statistic so that the agents cannot invent numbers, and a test agent runs the planned tests for the four hypotheses. After that, a discovery agent scans the responses for problems we did not think to ask about, such as low-rated user groups, answers that contradict the logs, the weakest pipeline stage and questions that people skip, and because these findings are exploratory, we confirm them on new responses before trusting them. Only anonymized data goes to a model, we make the final decisions ourselves, and we reduce the main threats, a non-random sample and self-report bias, by checking answers against the logs. In conclusion, the study will give us evidence on outcomes and acceptance. Thank you, we are ready for questions.
